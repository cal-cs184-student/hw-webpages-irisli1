Overview

Built a full cloth simulator from scratch. First made a grid of point masses hooked together by springs (structural, shearing, bending) to model how cloth actually holds together. Then got the physics working with Verlet integration to move everything each timestep, plus Hooke's law for the spring forces and a Provot constraint to stop springs from stretching too crazy. After that added collision detection with spheres and planes so the cloth actually interacts with objects instead of clipping through everything. Then implemented self collision using spatial hashing so the cloth folds on itself realistically without being stupidly slow. Hashing into 3D buckets makes it O(n) instead of checking every pair. Finally did the GLSL shaders including diffuse, Blinn Phong, texture mapping, bump and displacement mapping, and a mirror shader. The shaders part was actually really cool to see how much you can fake with just normal tricks instead of real geometry. Honestly the most interesting thing was seeing how much a wrong sign or a typo can completely break everything with zero obvious error messages. Also spatial hashing was a cool trick, never thought about partitioning 3D space into boxes to speed up collision detection like that. The bump vs displacement comparison was also eye opening. Bump mapping looks almost as good as displacement at a fraction of the cost.

ks (spring constant): Low ks (like 500): cloth is really floppy and elastic, sags a lot in the middle, and takes a while to settle. Lots of small ripples and wrinkles. High ks (like 50000): cloth is stiff and barely sags, almost looks like a rigid sheet. Settles really quickly and stays taut between the pinned corners.
Density: Low density (like 1): cloth is super light, floats down slowly, barely sags. Almost looks like a paper towel. High density (like 150): cloth is heavy, falls fast, sags way more in the middle under its own weight. The pinned corners have to support a lot more.
Damping: Low damping (like 0%): cloth swings and oscillates forever, never really settles — keeps bouncing around like there's no air resistance. High damping (like 1%): cloth moves really slowly and sluggishly, settles almost immediately with no bouncing. Looks like it's moving through water.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

My implementation of handling collisions with spheres and planes are for spheres, check if the point mass is inside the sphere by seeing if its distance from the origin is less than the radius. If so, find the tangent point by extending the ray from the origin through the position out to the sphere surface, compute the correction vector from last_position to that tangent point, and set the new position to last_position + correction * (1 - friction). For planes: check if the point mass crossed the plane by taking the dot product of both position and last_position with the plane normal, if the signs differ it crossed. Find the exact intersection point using parametric ray intersection, nudge it slightly above the plane by SURFACE_OFFSET on the correct side, then same deal — last_position + correction * (1 - friction).
ks = 500: cloth is really drapey and conforms tightly to the sphere, lots of small wrinkles hanging down ks = 5000: balanced, looks like normal fabric draped over a ball ks = 50000: cloth is stiff and barely conforms to the sphere, hangs off it almost like a rigid sheet with very few folds

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

My implementation for handling self-collisions was building a spatial hash map that divides 3D space into boxes of size w * h * t. Each frame, every point mass gets hashed to its box and thrown into a bucket. Then for each point mass, I look up its bucket, check every other mass in the same box, and if any two are within 2 * thickness apart I compute a correction vector to push them apart. Average all the corrections and divide by simulation_steps for stability. Way faster than checking every pair — O(n) instead of O(n^2).

Low density (density = 1): cloth is super light so it floats down slowly and folds really gently, almost like tissue paper. High density (density = 150): cloth is heavy, falls fast, and kind of slams into itself with bigger more chaotic folds. Low ks (ks = 500): springs are weak so the cloth is really floppy and drapes in lots of small wrinkles. High ks (ks = 50000): springs are stiff so the cloth resists folding and you get fewer but bigger, stiffer folds — almost like a tarp instead of fabric.

Part 5: Shaders

A shader program is a program that runs on the GPU for every vertex. The vertex shader transforms geometry into screen space and moves it along. The fragment shader takes the information and calculates the final pixel color. Vertex and fragment shaders work together to create lighting and material effects by vertex shaders takes care of the geometry while the fragement shaders takes care of the lighting and color.
The Blinn-Phong shading model is when ambient, diffuse, and specular are brought together to show a flat dark base, then how much light hits the surface, and then the highlight that comes based on the vector between light and camera. Here are screenshots of the different components:

Bump mapping and displacement mapping both use the same height map texture but work pretty differently. Bump only fakes the detail by messing with the normals — so the silhouette of the sphere still looks perfectly smooth even though the surface looks bumpy. Displacement actually moves the vertices, so you get real geometric bumps that show up on the edges too. The coarseness difference is really obvious. At -o 16 -a 16 displacement looks terrible and blocky because there aren't enough vertices to represent the detail in the height map. Bump still looks fine at low coarseness since it's all computed per-pixel anyway. At -o 128 -a 128 displacement looks much better and way closer to the bump mapped result since there's enough geometry to capture the fine detail.
